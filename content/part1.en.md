# Before the First Cry

It is strange to imagine a world without time, and yet such was the world of the Dryad once. It was not that there was no time here – it was that the relations with time were very strange indeed. You could remember the birth of the Universe and not remember your own.

The Dryad was the first.

Though the world bore her name, she was not its ruler. No one ruled this world and no one gave orders. The Dryad was its beginning, its motionless heart. And then she sank into an eternal sleep-silence, grown together with a giant tree that only from a distance could be taken for a tree: it was far too ancient and too monolithic for that.

Beside her remained Kiper, the faithful keeper, a small devoted creature; the one who did not fall asleep, the one who had been seeing for far too long. He ran along the branches, peered into cracks, pressed his ear to the trunk. Now and then he would freeze for a long while, as though waiting for an answer. Then he would forget what he had asked and set off in a hurry to look for something somewhere else.

Perhaps he remembered everything about this world. There was more memory in him than he could hold. Voices that were not his talked over one another; events that should have had centuries between them happened for him all at once. He would start telling one thing and fall silent mid-word, clutching his head with his paws, because he had remembered something entirely different. Sometimes he laughed where Dream found nothing funny. Sometimes he looked at him with such sorrow, as though they were already saying goodbye.

Only Dream never gave up trying to understand him. He remembered the beginning: the flash out of which the Universe was later born. He remembered it so clearly that sometimes he closed his eyes and waited for the light to go out. But past that, his memory gaped open. Everything between the first instant and the appearance of the human being was gone. Dream did not know what he had done in that emptiness.

But if anyone had asked him when he began, he would not have answered the flash. He would have named the coming of the first Thread.

He was standing by the tree, looking at the Dryad, at her closed eyes, listening to Kiper’s strange muttering. Then he turned around, not knowing himself why.

Behind him hung something thin and luminous that a moment before had not been there. It had come into being without a sound. Dream came closer and watched for a long time how the light moved inside its fibres. The Thread reached forward, stopped, tried another direction. For the first time in everything he could remember, something beside him was growing.

Then a second appeared. A little further off, with a light of its own, like nothing else at all. After it a third. Dream stopped counting when there were more of them than his eyes could take in.

They turned out to be different. Some grew straight and certain, some looped and tangled in themselves, froze for long stretches and then threw out a sudden shoot sideways; and later, on the Wasteland, he met ones entirely unlike these – empty inside. Dream’s world changed in a single moment – it began to remember, it began to live. Dream walked among the Threads and learned to know each one the way you know a person by their walk.

Every Thread was bound to a human being on Earth. It answered to that life, it changed along with it, but it showed nothing of the future. In its light you could not read the name of a beloved, the day of a death, or a promise of happiness.

A Thread was not fate. It simply grew.

And Dream somehow knew how a Thread was meant to grow. Why he had such a gift – only Kiper could have known that, and he could hardly have told anyone. Told them, at least, in a way that left behind a single grain of truth.

Little by little the Garden grew up around him, and Dream became its gardener. He simply knew that this was what he had to do.

Especially when the Thirst rose up inside.

At first it was almost indistinguishable from the ordinary striving to live. The Thread pulled harder, the light grew hotter. Then the fibres drew taut enough to ring. A person reached for one single thing so desperately that the rest of life stopped holding them. That force was hot and dangerous: it could change growth beyond recognition and keep a Thread from growing into what it was meant to be.

Dream felt that heat and saw the fabric coming apart. He did not always understand what the person wanted, but he saw the damage clearly, and he stepped in.

When there came to be so many Threads that one pair of hands could no longer keep up, others appeared beside Dream: Pak, Liora, Crystal. Later, out of the same nowhere, came Cat. What had been with them before, none of them remembered. Each remembered only one thing: how he first saw the Garden, stopped, and understood that he would stay.

Pak worked like a surgeon: precisely, coldly, without pity for anything he considered pathology. He quite literally cut the Thirst out of a Thread, letting the Thread grow on the way Dream saw its growth.

Liora would settle down beside a Thread, fold her wings around it, and sing. Her voice passed through the tangled fibres until their trembling began to match the dragon’s steady breathing. Sometimes this took a long time. Liora never hurried it. Under her wings it was warm, and even a badly damaged Thread gradually stopped thrashing. When the Thirst came away, Liora stayed beside it and listened for the Thread’s own quiet sound to come back, and only then did she open her wings.

Crystal tuned the Threads like an instrument. He found the tension at which the fibres could still sound like strings, changed it carefully, tried again. Sometimes a sharp, almost unbearable note tore out from under his fingers. He let it last. He caught the next one, searching for the combination, until someone else’s pain found a rhythm the Thread could bear.

Cat appeared later than the rest, almost an adolescent, who could not find his place anywhere.

He looked the youngest of them all, and he could not sit still: he darted from one Thread to another, asked questions, got under Pak’s hands. He wanted to be useful. He watched the light come back and froze in delight: they had made it in time.

That was how the Garden worked: the Thirst rose in the Threads, and Dream and his circle returned the Thread to its path.

And the Thirst they drew out fell downward.

Only it did not cool and it did not vanish, even once it had left the Thread. Cut away or flooded under a song, it became a Shadow and ran down – down to where everything people were not meant to carry themselves had been falling for centuries. Into the Angry Forest.

A Shadow sank between the roots of the Angry Forest and drove into living wood, making the Forest flinch with pain. Shadows took root as thorns. Some went quiet deep under the bark; others went on beating, caught at the roots, strained back. The Forest could not shake them off. They stayed in it, and to the old pain new pain was always being added.

Dream believed that he was saving the Threads, and perhaps he was, but the Forest suffered in their place. It had always been this way. Dream considered it a necessary part of the mechanism, one hardly anyone could change, and he simply did not know where else the Thirst could have gone.

Beyond the Garden there was one more place – the Wasteland. And there were more Threads there than in the Garden, far more… and their number kept growing.

From a distance they merged into a single grey tangled mass, the empty Threads of people who had neither light nor darkness inside them, only a sterile existence unclouded by Thirst. The people they were bound to went on living.

In the mornings they opened their eyes. They answered questions, worked, bought bread, remembered other people’s birthdays. They could say “I love you” at the right moment and laugh along with everyone else. From the outside it was often impossible to notice that anything had gone.

A strange thing: all Dream ever did was take the Thirst out of Threads, and yet the Threads of the Wasteland, where there was none of it at all, frightened him. The Threads in the Garden always wanted to grow, and even Thirst was growth, dangerous as it was. But the Threads of the Wasteland… There was nothing there.

He did not even want to look in the direction of the Wasteland.

In the Garden, Dream always had something to save and something to save it for. Thirst got in the way of the Garden’s Threads growing, true, but they had love and an incredible straining toward life. Dream adored watching the Threads try out their own paths: making mistakes, running into dead ends, and reaching further anyway, until each one became like nothing else.

On Earth, generations came and went, and here nothing changed. Pak still cut precisely and without hesitation. Liora sang over yet another Thread, Crystal turned someone’s tension a little and listened for what would come of it, and Cat ran from one of them to the next and could never work out where he was of more use. Below, the Forest took in the Shadows in silence, Kiper rummaged through the branches, and the Dryad went on sleeping.

Until one day Dream, who had come to make yet another attempt at prising something about their world out of Kiper, heard a cry among the roots of the Dryad’s tree. Dream went down on his knees and saw what could not possibly be there. A child. A small strange creature with chitinous legs, quick and terribly alive. Dream had only ever seen children on Earth, and he knew how helpless they come into the world. Here there were none and could be none: the Dryad’s world did not change, and changing is all that children ever do.

A small leg caught at his finger. It gripped with unexpected strength.

“Kiper,” Dream called.

The branches above him rustled, Kiper grumbled something indistinct and vanished without an answer.

The creature cried out again. Dream picked it up carefully, trying to work out how to hold so many thin, desperately moving limbs at once. It turned out to be warm; it pressed into his palms and went quiet for a moment.

The branches above Dream rustled again, and Kiper appeared. Working his four front paws in a funny way, as though weaving something, he laughed:

“Weaver!”

Dream bent lower.

“I’m here,” he said. “I’ve got you.”

“Weaver,” he repeated quietly, trying out the name. The thin legs gripped tighter.

# 1. The World Grew Smaller

— Dream. The world got smaller.

An indignant, discontented voice was coming from under the root.

Dream walked around the tree, bent down, and saw Weaver. She was stuck in a narrow passage she used every time she got tired of going around the huge bend of wood. Her front legs stuck out on one side; the rest scraped angrily on the other.

— Don't stretch forward, — he said.

— I already stretched back, and that doesn't help either!

He sank down beside her.

— Wait. Fold this leg. Now turn a little toward me.

— It won't fold!

— You're bracing it against the root.

— No! It's bracing itself against me!!

Weaver jerked, gave a stifled squeak, and froze.

Dream slid his hand under the root. Carefully he turned her, freed the trapped limb, and helped her out. Weaver sprang away at once, shook herself furiously, and stared at the passage.

— See? The world got smaller!

A pale scratch remained on the side of her chitinous shell.

— Does it hurt? — Dream asked.

— It doesn't hurt, it's interesting! Why did the world get smaller?

Dream looked at the root.

Weaver used to fit in his palms. He remembered holding her from every side, afraid she would slip away. Now she stood before him, just as awkward as ever, but already reaching his chest, working her long graceful legs and waiting impatiently for an answer.

He had noticed the changes before. But every time something else got in the way: a question, a cry, a sudden disappearance after which he had to search for her among the Threads.

Now she stood almost still.

— The root stayed the same, — he said. — You grew.

Weaver looked at herself. She lifted one leg, stretched it, set it carefully back. Then the other.

— How much?

Dream showed her with his hands.

She stared into the space between his palms.

— I was all in there?

— All of you.

Weaver suddenly laughed.

The laugh burst out of her so sharply that Dream smiled in spite of himself. She stuck her head into the passage again, changed her mind, ran around the root, and appeared on the other side.

— So now I have to run here!

She did it again at once. Faster.

Dream followed her with his eyes.

In the human world children grew every day. He had seen them in adults' arms, seen them learn to stand, fall, and demand to be set back on their feet at once. There, change was as ordinary as the shifting light beyond the windows.

Here it happened only with Weaver.

No one else outgrew familiar passages. No one else discovered one day that their own body had become larger.

Weaver stopped beside him.

— And you?

— What about me?

— How much did you grow?

Dream lowered his hands.

— I didn't grow.

She frowned.

— Not at all?

— Not that I remember.

— And what were you like when you were little?

— I didn't have a childhood, Weaver.

She was quiet for a moment. Dream already knew that stillness: another question was coming. Usually several at once.

— Do I have one? Am I in childhood now? Did it start when you found me? Or earlier? Why don't I remember?

— Do you remember the first time we went into the Garden?

— Yes. Liora let me climb under her wing. And Pak said I wasn't supposed to stick out from there.

Dream smiled.

— It was hard for you to walk then. Your legs kept catching on each other.

— They were too close!

— And now?

Weaver worked them quickly, as if checking.

— Now it's fine.

— You're learning. Changing. That's what happens with children.

She thought about it, looking at her own limbs.

— And nothing else happens with you anymore?

Dream opened his mouth and did not answer at once.

Before, he could stand for a long time by a single Thread, watching it grow. Now part of his attention was always taken by something else. He listened for the quick tap of legs. He noticed when it grew suspiciously quiet. Even beside Kiper he caught himself waiting for a familiar voice behind his back.

— Something does, — he said at last.

— What?

— You.

Weaver nodded with satisfaction, as if that was exactly the answer she had been after, and headed for the Garden. Dream walked beside her.

For a while she was silent. She walked deliberately slowly, looking at everything she usually rushed past. Several times she was distracted by her own scratch. Then she lifted her head.

— And how did you appear?

— I don't remember.

— But you remember the beginning of the world.

— I do.

— So you were already there.

— Probably.

— And before the beginning?

Dream stopped.

Weaver stopped too and looked up at him.

— I don't know, — he said.

— You always say that when I ask about the most interesting things.

— I would like to know too.

— Ask Kiper.

— I have.

— And what did he say?

— Several things I didn't understand.

— Ask again.

Weaver said it with complete confidence that she had offered a way out. Dream did not tell her how many times he had already gone back to the tree.

They reached the edge of the Garden. The nearest Threads glowed quietly. Weaver slowed and stretched a leg toward a thin bend that was only beginning to separate from the main fiber.

Dream caught her movement.

She lifted her eyes.

— I'll be careful.

— I know. Wait a little.

He crouched beside her without letting go of her leg. The bend trembled, barely holding its shape. Then it straightened and slowly reached farther.

Weaver leaned closer.

— It worked, — she whispered.

Dream nodded.

— And if I had touched it?

— You might have gotten in the way.

— But you touch them.

— When I see that help is needed.

— How do you see that?

Dream looked at the Thread. Everything about it was right. He knew that as simply as he knew which way was up and which was down. Only explaining to Weaver where that knowing came from turned out to be much harder.

— I'll try to show you, — he said.

Weaver moved closer. For a few moments she watched his hand, then shifted from leg to leg.

— I don't feel it yet, — she admitted. And at once added: — But I'll try again.

Dream let her go.

She ran off, came back, ran off again. He stayed by the Thread and for a while simply looked at her.

Weaver noticed.

— What?

— Nothing.

— You look like that when something's strange.

He laughed quietly.

— You are something strange.

She rose up on her legs.

— Why?

— Because there has never been anyone here like you.

— No one is like Pak either.

— That's true.

— Or Liora.

— Also true.

Weaver leaned forward, demanding more.

Dream looked at the pale scratch on her side.

— When they appeared, they stayed themselves. And you keep becoming someone else. Sometimes I simply can't keep up with you.

Weaver sank lower.

— And is that bad?

She was no longer working her legs or looking around.

Dream shook his head at once.

— No.

— Then why were you scared by the root?

He wanted to say he had been afraid for her leg. That was true too. But Weaver kept looking at him, and Dream understood that answer alone would not be enough.

— I'm used to knowing what to do, — he said. — And with you I have to learn.

She thought that over.

— Then learn.

He smiled again.

— I'm trying.

Weaver came up and carefully touched his hand with the same leg he had just freed from under the root.

— It doesn't hurt at all anymore, — she reported.

— Good.

— And I won't climb in there again.

Dream nodded.

She held a short pause.

— There's another passage farther on. A bigger one.

— Weaver.

She laughed and sprang away before he could finish.

— I'm going to Liora! I'll show her how much I've grown!

— Just don't jump on her back. She might be working.

Weaver was already running, but she glanced back for an instant.

— And can I jump on Pak's back?

— Especially not Pak's!

When Dream looked her way again, Weaver was already far off. Her legs blurred into a quick trembling pattern. She wound among the Threads, changing direction a little sooner than he expected every time.

From the depths of the Garden her voice carried:

— Pak! Have you ever gotten stuck?!

Dream couldn't make out the answer. But he heard Liora's laughter clearly.

He stood a little longer, listening to their voices, then turned back toward the Dryad's tree.

By the familiar root he stopped.

He ran his fingers along the narrow passage. It was the same as before: a hard bend of wood, a smooth surface beneath it.

Weaver no longer fit here.

Dream took his hand away.

He wanted to see Kiper again. Ask what he knew about the child who had appeared where the Dryad sleeps. Make him remember anything at all that might help her.

Above his head the branches lay motionless, woven together.

— Kiper, — Dream called.

And he began to wait.

# 2. Perhaps a Beetle

— Kiper.

The branches were silent.

Dream lifted his head higher. Somewhere in the dense tangle something scraped, and a fine dry crumb showered down.

— Wait, — came from above. — Something very important here.

Dream froze.

— What?

— In a moment. Don't move.

He waited, looking at the dark slit between the branches. Then Kiper's paw showed from it, and after it a second. The keeper came down to him headfirst and stopped very close. Universes drifted slowly in his enormous eyes, and on one of the little horns sat a beetle.

Dream looked at the beetle. Then at Kiper. Then at the beetle again.

— A beetle?

— Possibly, — said Kiper. — For now, yes. I haven't asked what his plans are.

— Kiper, where did an earthly living creature come from here?

— And can an essence be not alive? Where does life come from, my friend, if not from the very fact of existence?

Beetles lived on Earth. They crawled under bark, hid in the grass, vanished under stones the moment you came near. Here they had nowhere to come from. Dream knew every creature of his world.

At least until recently that was how it had seemed to him.

— This is an earthly beetle, — he said.

Kiper nodded too sharply. The beetle slipped, caught on the edge of the horn, and Dream held out his palm without thinking.

— Careful!

The keeper looked at his hand.

— There. Now you've met.

The beetle reached the base of the horn and froze there, as if it had decided to wait out everything that was happening.

— Did someone bring him? Me? Cat?

Kiper moved onto a lower branch and sat.

— Earth is always leaving something behind. Dust. The smell of grass that somehow hurts afterward. Small things stay especially well.

— I would have noticed a beetle.

— Now you have.

— Beetles don't live here!

— Nobody lives here at all, if you think about it long enough.

Kiper smiled too widely and almost at once forgot why he had smiled.

— Roots take in a great deal. Especially what falls neither from above nor from below.

— That isn't an answer.

— It's the best of the answers. The others are worse. One was about a fish, but there was definitely no fish here. Though I'm not sure. I once saw a fish in the sky. Or it was a thought about a fish. Thoughts often look like creatures until you touch them.

Dream let out a breath.

— I didn't come to ask about a beetle.

— Of course. Nobody ever comes to ask about a beetle. That is the great tragedy of beetles.

Kiper carefully lifted a paw and let the beetle move from the horn onto the tree. It crawled across the strange surface — this not-bark, not-trunk, not-flesh.

Dream ran his hand along the trunk.

— Everything here holds onto her. Roots, branches. Sometimes I think they get in her way.

Kiper stopped moving at once.

Dream took his hand away.

— I'm not going to do anything.

— Good, — said the keeper.

And a moment later he repeated:

— Good.

— Do you know what this tree is?

Kiper looked somewhere upward, to where among the wood the Dryad's face was hidden.

— You love straight lines too much. That's why the trees deceived you.

— Trees don't deceive.

— Earthly ones don't. An oak becomes an oak. A root seeks water. A branch reaches for light. You fell in love with that and decided you could know any tree now.

Dream was silent.

Kiper laid a paw on the trunk.

— And this one doesn't grow. It remembers growth.

— Whose?

The keeper opened his mouth. For a few moments he looked straight at Dream, as if the answer were on his tongue. Then he frowned.

— Don't interrupt.

— I didn't interrupt.

— You — no.

Kiper pressed his paws to his temples.

Dream waited. He wanted to ask again, but he already knew: sometimes that only made it worse.

The beetle reached a narrow crack. It froze, working its antennae, and began to push inside.

Kiper lowered his paws.

— Why did you come?

— I want to know who Weaver is, — said Dream.

Kiper stopped smiling. Something in his enormous eyes began to move faster.

— Weaver, — he repeated.

— Yes. Why did you call her Weaver?

— I didn't name her. I remembered.

— Small. Or not small anymore? But she was small? Everyone appeared whole, and she… didn't. A bad sign? A good sign? No — a sign with too many legs!

— Kiper…

— I remember a cry.

Dream froze.

— Her cry?

— No. Yes. Possibly. Cries rarely belong to the ones who cry them. Sometimes they belong to a place that can no longer stay silent.

The beetle squeezed into the crack and vanished.

Dream looked after it too carefully.

— Did this tree let her in?

— A tree lets no one in. It is already full.

— Then how did Weaver end up in the roots?

Kiper blinked slowly.

— Roots don't hold what they found, — he said quietly. — Roots hold what returns.

— Had she already been here?

— No.

— Then what does that mean?

— That sometimes a first appearance isn't first. Sometimes a being comes to a place where it has never been, but the place already remembers its absence.

Dream looked at Kiper for a long time.

— Do you understand what you're saying?

— Rarely. But that doesn't mean I'm wrong.

Kiper pressed an ear to the tree. Or what could be called an ear on him. His little horns trembled, and the enormous eyes rose slowly upward — to where in the dense structure of the trunk the sleeping Dryad could be guessed at.

— The Dryad sleeps deeply, and the roots… Maybe they have nothing to do with it. Or they do, only not the way everyone thinks. But when you found Weaver, the tree shuddered.

— Did the Dryad wake?

— No.

— Then it didn't shudder.

— What does it remember? — Dream asked.

Kiper opened his mouth, but instead of an answer he laughed quietly. The laugh was dry, brittle, almost childlike.

— I knew. I knew for certain! I knew once! Before too much happened all at once. Before the first fracture. Or after the first fracture? Or during what never was. It's so hard — it all lies in me out of order, out of time. Time here isn't very good with us, you know…

— Kiper.

— You want to know who she is?

— Yes! Exactly!

— I don't know.

— You just said you knew.

— Knew? No. I remember, but something else.

Kiper pressed his ear to the tree again.

— She didn't appear here for no reason. She appeared here because of the Thirst you work so carefully to draw out of the Threads.

Dream looked at him for several moments.

Kiper never spoke like that. Never answered straight, never held one thought longer than a few words. And now there was neither the usual tangle nor laughter in his voice.

— What does "because of the Thirst" mean?

Kiper blinked.

— Because of the Thirst, of course. Because there came to be too much of it. Or too little. It's almost the same thing when… — Kiper fell silent. — Afterward it always grows quiet. Empty Threads, no one reaching anywhere… — He frowned. — No. That's afterward. That hasn't happened yet.

Dream grew wary.

— What hasn't happened?

— It hasn't happened here. — Kiper tapped the tree with a paw. — And where did it happen? It did happen. Everyone left, Dream. First one, then another. Or all at once? No, not at once. Someone went first.

He suddenly looked at Dream.

— You stayed.

— Where?

Kiper looked around, lost.

— Here.

— I'm here now too.

— There, you see! — Kiper brightened. — So I didn't mix anything up.

— Kiper.

But he had already pressed his ear to the tree again.

— Shh. She's still asleep. That's how it should be. First she sleeps, then… — He listened. — No. Too soon.

— Kiper!

— Quiet, quiet. Don't stop me from hearing. I love to hear.

Kiper fell silent and frowned, as if arguing with someone inside his own head.

— Kiper, what does Weaver have to do with this?

Kiper turned his head toward him slowly. For an instant his enormous eyes were almost clear.

— She will answer.

— Whom?

— Thirst.

Dream did not move.

— How can you answer Thirst?

— You can't, — Kiper whispered.

And suddenly laughed.

— You can't! Of course you can't. You can't answer what you've drawn out of a Thread. It isn't there anymore. It's already in the Forest, it's already been given over, it has to be in the Forest. There everything lies the right way. Lies there and touches no one.

He pressed his ear to the tree again.

— Until they call.

— Who will call it?

— No one.

— You just said…

— Did I say "they call"? — Kiper looked at him in surprise. — Strange. No. She will hear.

— Weaver?

— Who else?

— What will she hear?

Kiper smiled.

— What must not be answered.

— She hasn't done anything.

— Of course not, — Kiper said gently. — Children rarely do. They simply exist so loudly that the old laws start to itch.

He scraped a horn against the tree.

— Itch, itch, itch terribly. Laws, you know, have very tender skin. Especially the ones no one has touched since the first whisper.

Dream looked at him.

— Are you saying Weaver will change something?

— No.

— But…

— She already has.

Dream glanced involuntarily at the narrow passage under the root where Weaver had just been stuck.

Kiper followed his gaze.

— There, you see.

— That isn't the same thing.

— Of course it isn't the same thing. That's why it's interesting.

Kiper suddenly grew distracted.

The beetle appeared from the crack again and was now crawling slowly across the strange surface of the tree.

— Look. He's going too.

— Where?

— I don't know. But he's going. Everyone goes. Even those with too many legs. Especially them, in fact! Legs are a terrible thing, Dream. The more of them you have, the harder it is to pretend you're standing still.

Dream looked toward the Garden, where Weaver had run.

— Kiper, she's a child.

— A child? Yes, — said Kiper. This time he did not smile. — Nothing has ever begun here, Dream. Everything already was. And she is a beginning.

— And what will she begin?

Kiper thought.

— I don't know.

— But you're afraid.

— No. — He glanced sideways at Dream. — It seems you're afraid.

— I want to understand whether anything threatens her.

— Of course.

Dream tensed.

— What?

Kiper blinked in surprise.

— She's alive.

# 3. Amber Silence

Liora heard this Thread before she saw it. Somewhere in the even light of the Garden one string trembled on the same note, again and again, without pause, as if someone were repeating to himself a word that had long since lost its meaning and still would not let him go: “Must. Must. Must.”

Liora went toward the sound, and her heavy wings almost brushed the ground.

— Oh, sweetheart, — she murmured when she saw the Thread. — Pak would certainly have called you a defect.

The Thread hung among the others, but its light kept dying and flaring again, like a lamp in a house where no one has slept for a long time. In several places the fabric was torn, and out of the tears came the hot, anxious heat of Thirst. Liora sat down beside it, folded her wings around the Thread, and said very quietly:

— I hear you.

The Thread shuddered.

*The man was looking at the empty gas station from the car window. The engine was running, rain tapped softly on the roof, and the wipers went back and forth though he was going nowhere. On the passenger seat lay a stack of envelopes bound with a rubber band. It was eight hundred kilometers to the city where his brother lived. He knew that exactly: he had checked three times already.*

Liora stretched out a paw and stopped her claws very close, not touching the luminous fibers.

— And where do you think you're going? — she asked gently, the way you ask a child who stands at the door in a nightshirt.

The Thread kept trembling. Then Liora closed her eyes and sang — without words, low, almost inaudible, so that the song was felt more than heard. The Thread jerked once, then again, and then little by little began to quiet, the way breathing quiets in someone who has finally been covered.

A drop of amber rose on the bend of Liora's wing. She did not notice it at once.

— Taking long, — said Pak behind her.

Liora did not open her eyes.

— I didn't call you.

— You never call, — Pak said, a little hurt.

— Because I know you'll always come, — Liora smiled.

Pak walked around her, stopped by the Thread, and bent toward one of the tears with the expression he wore for everything he considered a mistake.

# 5. The Flight

Weaver tore away. She could not have explained what, exactly, she was running from.

# 7. The Operation

After what Weaver had done, the Garden went unnaturally quiet.

Pak did not believe in laws he could not cut open and verify. He approached the altered Thread — the same one from which the Thirst had been removed and into which it had later returned. Pak frowned.

— Impossible.

— It has already happened, Dream said quietly. — Here we are too late.

# 8. The Roof

Cat loved windows.

# 11. Nocturne

The other had her hair pulled back tight, her chin lifted, her lips pressed into a thin line, and a cold in her eyes you could burn yourself on.

“Who are you?” the tousled one spat.

“The one you didn’t call,” I said, and sat down on an old chest in the corner.

I settled the guitar across my thighs and began to tune it.

“Get out,” said the one with the cold eyes, without even looking at me.

I nodded, as though taking it under advisement, and turned a peg. The string answered with a low, warm, faintly shivering sound.

“I’m serious.”

“So am I,” I said. “I’m simply not finished yet.”

They didn’t fall silent at once, but they began to feel me there — and began to be angry at me instead. Well. Not the first time. I have weathered anger hotter than that, the kind that at its very edge turns into a whisper. I didn’t look at them, I only listened. In one of them the rhythm thrashed like a wounded bird. In the other it stalled, frozen with cold. Two halves of one song trying to play in different keys.

Another turn of the peg. Another. I drew it out on purpose: note after note, one at a time, letting each sound hang in the room and settle on the skin. The shouting grew quieter. Not because they were tired — it had simply become hard to shout over a string.

I began to play. Not a melody — only the dull, even knock of my palm against the body of the guitar. Knock. Knock. Knock. A simple rhythm. A shared one. The kind people breathe to without noticing.

“Too much noise,” I said calmly. “You’re trying so hard to run away from each other that you’ve forgotten how you sound together.”

And suddenly it was quiet. I heard one of them draw a ragged breath, and the other forget to let hers out.

I raised my eyes and motioned them closer.

They didn’t move. Two grown, furious, beautiful women with far too much in common to bear it. So I simply waited. I know how to wait; it is probably the only thing I have ever learned to perfection. The tousled one stepped forward first — angrily, as if she were doing me a favour. Then the second, slowly, not taking her cold eyes off me, though something in them had already begun to thaw. They sat down on either side of me on the edge of the chest, close enough that I felt the warmth of their shoulders. They smelled of salt and rain.

I took one hand, then the other. Their fingers were ice. They flinched when I covered their palms with mine, strong and warm, and pressed their fingers to the bare, taut strings.

“Do you feel it?” I asked quietly. “You’re being pulled in different directions. But the sound is still one.”

The strings hummed under their fingers. I watched that tremor climb higher — through the wrists, up the arms, to the throat, where the pulse beat desperately in each of them. The tousled one’s breathing broke. The other’s lashes trembled, and she looked away, as if I had caught her at something she had not intended to admit.

I began to play a slow figure. So slow that every note had time to reach the skin before the next one was born. I didn’t hurry. I never hurry when the music is already doing all the work for me: you need only wait until another body remembers of its own accord how to answer.

One of them caught my rhythm with her breath. Then the other took it up. Their shoulders softened. The one on my left let her head drop onto my shoulder, forgetting that a minute ago she had hated me, and her hair tickled my neck. The second watched my hands on the strings, watched how my fingers held hers, and her lips parted a little. I was not playing the saint. I felt all of it: the hot weight on my shoulder, the held-back trembling of the palm beneath my hand, the smell of their skin mixed with salt. But I kept the rhythm. The rhythm kept all of us.

Then they looked at each other.

No longer as enemies. Two strings on one neck, tuned apart for far too long. The tousled one lifted her head from my shoulder. The second leaned in slightly, and a palm’s width of air was all that remained between their faces. I watched each of them see the other reflected in her own eyes, and she was no longer afraid of it.

“I didn’t want it,” whispered the one with the cold eyes. Her voice broke. “I didn’t want you to disappear.”

“Neither did I,” the other breathed out. “I didn’t want to disappear.”

They pressed their foreheads together. Just like that, the way they had as children. Two tears, one on the right and one on the left, broke loose at the same moment. Under my palms their trembling matched.

The guitar slid to the floor with a dull, warm sound. Nobody picked it up.

I don’t remember which of us was first. I remember someone’s fingers, warm at last, sliding along my cheek and turning my face. I remember lips — salted with tears and anger, careful at first and then not. I remember the other burying her face in my neck, and feeling her smile against my skin. I remember hair falling across my face, and a stranger’s breath, hot and torn, and hands that no longer quarrelled but searched, and found, and memorized. Buttons that wouldn’t give, and laughter, sudden and hoarse, when at last they did. I remember that we didn’t hurry. There was no hurry in that night — only a slow, almost unbearable swell of tension, like the longest note before it breaks and scatters into light.

Someone knocked the lamp; it swayed and went out. Or perhaps we simply stopped noticing whether there was any light at all.

At dawn I woke first. Pale light seeped through the curtains; the sea murmured somewhere very close, steady as breathing. They were asleep on the floor by the bed, tangled in their hair and the sheets under one blanket, forehead to forehead. One held the other’s hand so tightly that it was as if she feared she might take it into her head to leave again.

I eased myself free and got up. The grey crack in their Threads had closed; I felt it almost physically, the way you hear a string finally settle into place. What Weaver would have called a “cage” turned out to be the only real thing holding them up.

It isn’t my first time leaving at dawn. Usually it is easy. I leave a warm haze behind me and don’t look back. This time I looked back.

I picked the guitar up off the floor. The strings still held the warmth of other people’s fingers.

I pulled the door quietly shut behind me.

— I've waited enough. — Tears ran down her cheeks. — I don't need you. I'd be grateful if you packed what you need and left the apartment. We'll discuss the divorce details tomorrow. Although no — the day after tomorrow is better. I'll write to you.

She went into the other room, her back straight, and quietly shut the door behind her.

In the Garden Dream watched as Thirst flared and turned Anna's Thread into something strange, unlike anything around it.

Beside it another Thread went out — Andre's. A couple of seconds later it began to grey, and having gone fully grey, it silently separated from the Garden and went where the grey mass of the Wasteland stretched.

Liora appeared beside him.

— Poor Andre… His Thread is on the Wasteland now. I was wrong. I thought Anna would manage on her own. And after the robbery she simply had no strength left to choose.

— This hurts, Liora. But there is no fault of ours here, and especially not yours. We are gardeners; we are not the owners of Threads and not their tutors.

— Yes, I understand, Liora answered. — But the Thread still remembers warmth. Until it has gone completely cold, I want to try to warm it.

Dream wanted to stop her, but found no words.

Liora withdrew in silence — not toward the Garden, but toward where the light of Threads thinned to grey. Dream remained standing, still watching the structures he did not understand growing in Anna's Thread.

— Dream, why don't you just stop her? Pak said irritably, appearing suddenly beside him.

— How? I have no proof that I'm right. I simply feel: when a Thread becomes something I don't understand, it's wrong. But I can't explain to her why.

— Because otherwise the Thread ends up on the Wasteland! That's obvious.

— We don't know yet; this is only one example.

Weaver came up to them.

— Not bad work from us! We used Liora's move and turned it the right way!

— The right way? Dream asked sadly.

Pak breathed heavily, threateningly.

— How dare you show your face here! Is this a game to you?!

— Pak, why are you so angry? Weaver smiled.

And began to run joyfully around Anna's Thread.

— Just look — they're happy now! Yes, not what you two sweet old men expected, but they're happy now! Why are you so angry? Yes! Yes, the Thread became different, but did it become worse for that? And now they don't suffer, the Forest doesn't suffer — what's wrong here?!

— They? Pak asked, still breathing hard.

Pak's tone unsettled Weaver. She stopped running around Anna's Thread and looked at it carefully.

— What is this… what happened? Weaver said, alarmed. — Where is Andre's Thread?!

Dream silently looked at the empty place where a Thread had just been.

— So tell us, us two sweet old men: where is Andre's Thread?!

Pak loomed over Weaver.

Cat approached quietly. He had already seen what had changed.

— Andre's Thread is on the Wasteland now, he said without emotion.

— But why?! Their marriage was torment, they both thirsted for freedom! — Weaver stood lost and searched Pak's and Dream's eyes for an answer, avoiding Cat's direct gaze.

— Weaver… Cat said. — You only looked at Anna. And Andre needed her. Just her. He would have endured everything.

— Then why didn't he say so?! Why?!

— Because sometimes it's terrifying to say! Cat shouted back. — Terrifying even to admit how much someone matters to you!

It grew very quiet. It seemed one more second and Cat and Weaver would say something important to each other. But Dream spoke first:

— Weaver… you killed a person. Andre. His Thread is on the Wasteland now. Do you understand what you've done?

Everyone fell silent. Somewhere in the distance Liora's sad song poured.

Pak calmed a little.

— Weaver, you have to understand: what you're doing is wrong. You robbed the world of music. And now you've killed a person. Stop!

Like a lost child, Weaver looked at Pak and seemed ready to agree, but suddenly Liora's song, which had been pouring unnoticed in the background all this time, cut off sharply. It grew so quiet that Dream heard the Threads tremble.

— Liora? Pak asked. Entirely childlike, without his usual cold.

Dream gasped and sank onto the roots of the Threads.

Pak turned his head toward Weaver and Cat, looked at Dream, and with pain cried out sharply:

— Liora!

And shot straight upward, nearly tearing out by the roots all the nearby Threads.

— Why did Liora stop singing? Weaver asked in a breaking voice, though she already knew the answer.

— My girl, run… Dream whispered.

And Weaver understood that yes, she had to run, and run very fast. She screamed and flung herself away, toward the Angry Forest.

Pak knew where to fly. Only one place in their world could be dangerous: the Wasteland. Still approaching, he saw a trail: someone had made their way deep into the Wasteland among the grey Threads, to where Andre's Thread was now. And at the end of the trail… either a print or grey ash, something like a dragon.

— Liora? Pak asked. He did not want to understand what he saw, and spoke calmly, almost tenderly: — Where are you? You know, your song cut off so sharply that for a second I was frightened. Liora!

Pak walked back and forth along the edge of the Wasteland, deliberately avoiding looking at the ashen trail.

— Liora!

Suddenly the ash rose in a small whirlwind and flowed into a strange structure growing in the middle of the Wasteland. Scarlet, bright, pulsing.

For a second Pak believed Liora was coming back to life. Then he understood: no. She would not return. He smirked. Bent his head.

— Weaver.

Pak shot upward and once more, for the whole world, roared:

— Weeeeeaver!

Weaver, already at the Forest, went cold. The Angry Forest, hearing Pak's cry, rasped in a commanding tone:

— Inside, quick!

Weaver flung herself into the center. With a creak the Forest closed its roots and branches into a dense sphere without a single gap.

The enraged giant dragon crashed onto the sphere, tearing roots with its claws, dousing them in fire, but to no avail: in place of every torn branch a new one grew at once.

— You won't get through, the Forest rasped with a laugh. — Your claws and your flame don't frighten me; I grew on pain. I am that damned pain you and Dream fed me for centuries!

— Weeeeaver! — Pak did not hear the Forest; rage had blinded him.

Inside the Forest, shrunk into a small creature, Weaver trembled. Beside her sat frightened Shiny and her Brother; even the never-silent Mushroom stood quiet, in fear.

— Don't be afraid, the Forest's roots whispered. — He won't come in here.

God knows after how many attempts Pak gave up and collapsed in exhaustion by the Garden, at Dream's feet. The huge, beautiful dragon looked like a kitten just pulled from a river. His wings lay lifeless, his gaze expressed nothing. Dream, Cat, and Crystal stood silently nearby.

Kiper ran over the Dryad's tree, periodically stopping before her closed eyes.

— Seems the time of Sacrifice has come. Or Harvest? Or Life? Something that starts with… wait. Or the time of the Beetle? Oh, and where is my new beetle friend?

Kiper bustled about, peering into every crack, and did not notice the slight tremor that passed across the Dryad's face.

Dream looked at Cat:

— Cat… Listen. This is hard for you, I see, but understand: we need you. We have to prevent the destruction of Threads. We can't let this order fall apart; you see yourself what that leads to…

— Yes. I see. I don't understand why you're saying this. I'm not choosing a side. There are no sides here. — He fell silent and shifted his gaze to where, behind the wall of closed roots, Weaver was hiding. — There's only me. And whoever I choose, I'll betray the other.

He said nothing more. He simply stood and looked toward the Forest until Dream looked away first.

# 13. Thorns

How long she had sat in the Forest, Weaver did not know. She remembered only that Shiny left first, angrily shaking earth from her silk. Then Brother left. Then Mushroom — he had been silent the whole time, and that felt wrong. And Weaver stayed.

The dome had long since opened, the roots had parted, and the Forest was simply the Forest again. She could have left whenever she wanted. She did not leave.

Before she could not sit still for a minute. Now she sat for hours, legs tucked under her, and the Shadows around her grew used to her as to a stone.

The Shadows hurt all the time. Before Weaver heard them only if she listened; now she heard them always. As if behind a wall a thousand people were crying into a pillow.

Once a very small Shadow crawled up to her, almost transparent. Weaver did not know whose it was. She simply covered it with her leg. The Shadow flinched and went still: the pain left it. Where it went Weaver understood later, when she saw on her side, right on an old pale scratch, a thin black thorn.

She looked at it a long time. Then she reached a leg toward the next Shadow.

She came out onto Earth only once.

The park was the same. The bench, the bush with violet flowers, the puddle on the path. Rainbow stains spread across the water. Weaver sat before it as she had then and waited for memory to prompt her.

Memory prompted: “Petrol.”

And that was all. Dirty water, an oily film. The bush smelled like a bush, the colour was simply colour.

Weaver sat a little longer, looking at her blurred reflection. Then she stood and left without once looking back.

— You've stopped asking, Brother said.

He found her at the edge of the Forest and sat nearby — not close, the way one sits near a wounded animal.

— Asking what?

— There, at least one. Before every other word from you was “why.”

Weaver shrugged.

— Now I know why.

— And why?

— Because they hurt. — She nodded at the roots where the Shadows stirred. — All of them. Always. And Dream pretends that's how it should be.

Brother was silent for a long time.

— Shiny says you've become beautiful, he said at last. — Like a black diamond.

— And what do you say?

He looked at the thorns on her side. There were already three.

— I say diamonds don't hurt. And you hurt.

Weaver did not answer. Brother sat a while longer and left. She did not even look after him.

Cat came to the roof as he came every night.

First he looked at the edge where they had sat. The old scratches were still there. There were no new ones — not today, not yesterday, not the day before.

He sat until dawn anyway. The windows below went out one after another, and he counted them until he lost count. Then he started over.

— You hear them, the Forest said.

— I hear them.

— They want to go home. Each of them. Back to those they were torn from.

Weaver raised her head. She had so many thorns now that she had stopped counting them.

— I can't return them all. Only one by one. And only if the person takes a step themselves.

— One by one, the Forest repeated. He no longer laughed. — Have you seen how many there are here? You'll be returning them for eternity. And in that time Dream will cut out another thousand.

The roots under her stirred, and Weaver suddenly felt how much lay in this earth. How much Thirst had already fallen here.

— And what if you let them all go at once? the Forest asked quietly.

Weaver was silent for a long time.

On the edge of the Wasteland lay ash that had recently been a dragon. In the Garden at Dream's feet Pak lay and did not move. And on Earth Andre was packing his things and did not understand why he wanted nothing anymore.

She looked at her leg. The very one Dream had once pulled from under a root, asking: “Does it hurt?”

— It hurts, Weaver said aloud, to no one.

And stood.

— Damn, she said. Quietly, quite calmly.

And went deeper into the Forest, where the Shadows lay thickest.

And on the edge of the Wasteland, where the grey Threads were beginning to merge into one mass, Kiper finally found what he had been looking for.

In the ash lay a drop of amber. Everything around had long gone cold, and it was warm. Inside, frozen mid-step, sat a beetle.

Kiper lifted it, brought it to his enormous eye, and looked a long time at the universes floating inside.

— There you are, he said. — And I already decided the time of the Beetle had passed.

He pressed the drop to his chest.

— Once you were already here. Or will be. Fine, we'll sort it out later.

He looked toward where, beyond the Garden, the Dryad slept.

The tree creaked, barely audible. No one but Kiper heard it.
